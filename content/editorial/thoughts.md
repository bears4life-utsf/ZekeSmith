When SAFe Stops Scaling — Interview Answers
(Tier 1 questions from the Story Brief — answer in your own words, unpolished.)

1. The moment I concluded the model had stopped scaling. When the amount of time required to do work was oftentimes exceeded by the planning and coordination of that work. When we started tracking output and metrics and numbers without seeming to care about outcomes and customer focus. I remember on many occasions how frustrated I would be when it was so hard to get anything changed. Customers who pay us tens of millions of dollars would request something and we'd have to go to the SAFE committee (as a VP) to get approval and sometimes they'd simply say no. It would make me mad because we had no recourse. That group was too powerful and everyone felt SAFE was forced on us. When creativity became beholden to process and everything came to a grinding halt. When the PMO who ran SAFE became too powerful and out of touch with what we really do and that is to solve customer problems. We were a slave to the process. I remember on many occasions how pissed I'd get when the SAFE committee would meet and wouldn't even include product. It was really really frustration. 

2. What I originally believed SAFe would solve — and when that changed. I believed it would solve the complex dependencies that existed between our codebases and our legacy software. It changed when we started to get way too many status in the process, and not enough individual ownership over actual outcomes. It also caused a lot of personal frustration and caused silos between product and engineering which was not helpful. It got less personal which is a net loss in our software business. We deal with insurance claims, and that is a very personal thing when someone has a house fire or loses everything. My job is to help those people, they rely on me. It made me sad when I had to tell them I couldn't so something for months. They didn't understand or care about our process. This made me feel jaded about SAFe. Part of this it not SAFE's fault but the way we implemented it. The focus should be oh helping customers solve problems, not following some process internally. Safe became too important. Safe seemed to ensure we started a lot or work, but we had so much work in progress. 

3. The fairest paragraph I can write about SAFe. It is a framework, just like many others. It has some good qualities like how it handles prioritization, some of the ceremonies are valuable although I personally felt burdened by them and they were just to overbearing. (not all, there are too many) but some. The idea to push coordination between different groups is noble, but it forced too many communication points and too many people had to attend too many meetings. Developers were always in meetings, always context switching and that’s expensive and frustrating. Developers hated it, they felt like this was something forced on them. 

4. Coordination work I underestimated when scaffolding came down. We lost some of the formal meetings and ceremonies and we’ve had to add back a few. But that was more of a result of us learning the product operating model, which we continue to do. I feel much better about helping set strategy and helping the team understand what problems to solve, and then letting them solve those problems. That allows me to focus on my role and not waste my time in hours and days of meetings that sometimes just seemed like theatre. 

5. What made .NET 8 / “safe-lite” feel like the old model returning. Let’s exclude this from the research. 

6. Best proof that ownership moved for real. We are not where I want to be yet, but ownership moved away from the PMO which wasn’t even part of the product or engineering team, to the actual product and engineering teams. We aren’t great yet, and I'm not happy with where we are yet, but we are pushing responsibility down to the teams, but also autonomy to the smaller teams. This is our current focus. There seemed to be no ownership at all with SAFE. The PMO had no ownership and I felt like I was always defending and fighting for my product team because we had no control over outcomes, so I pushed back on taking responsibility as well. That is a bad bad place to be. I'd rather focus on extreme ownership, but without having the ability to control outcomes, that is a losing battle. 

7. Survey struggle that bothers me most. Let’s exclude this. 
 
Fragments / observations
Safe moved product and engineering away from the product and those groups became beholden to the process. In the end, the process needs to be a means to the end, not the end itself. Customer outcome should always come first and not simply output. We are building solutions and experiences; this is not a factor where we churn out widgets. 

Things I Never Want To Forget
Every operating model eventually optimizes for something. Make sure it's the customer.
Customers don't care about our operating model, they just want their problems solved.
Processes exists to help solve customer problems. Full stop. 
The closer decisions are to customers, the better they become.
Ownership without authority creates learned helplessness.
Every company is different, so should be the processes. 
If you do promote individual ownership, you will eventually lose.

Philosophy of Leadership — Interview Notes
(Raw. Not an essay. Source for the manifesto and later articles. Captured September 2026.)

Public rule, locked: the essays can show the gray-area moves and the line that makes them defensible. Names, accounts, and live deals stay off the page. That difference is the point.

Where it came from
Jeff Taylor mentored me early. Small company, about 30 people. He taught politics, incentives, power. His lines I still use: it doesn't matter if you are the CEO or the janitor as long as your mop is made of gold — don't get caught up in the office and the parking spot, remember you are here to make money. Tie yourself to the money. Get on the best projects. A lower seat on a moneymaker beats a higher seat on a loser. If the new thing is the future, tie yourself to that too. And if your baby is ugly, say so. Scott told me to thrive in change. When change hits I re-read the power structure, the politics, the incentives, and my value to the right people. I have had to reinvent that value more than once.

First time leading a person
Around 1999 or 2000 I was in QA. Manual cases in Excel. I got a screen-record tool (Visual Test, I think), showed initiative, and they put me in charge. The person who had led QA then reported to me. I told him I was not hunting his job, that I valued what he taught me, and that I would not let the change look bad on him. He was a great individual contributor and a hard worker. He did not love leading. I made him senior and put him on our biggest account. He excelled and later retired as an IC. I never devalue a good IC. They make the company go. I push salary, titles, bonuses, and I say their work out loud. They change my mind.

Ribbon, about 2010
I wanted things buried in menus. I knew where they were. A developer said nobody wants to hunt menus. I didn't listen. Customers hated it. We ripped it out and moved toward a ribbon. My taste was one data point, and a bad one, because I was not a new user. Since then I try to challenge my own opinion before I challenge others. UX usually wins because they know UX better than I do. Not always. On the online redesign, a long list of items from a home fire: UX put search at the top, so you scrolled down to pick an item and back up to search. I wanted the search inline, on the item. I didn't order it. I asked for both mocks and customer feedback. Customers picked inline.

When there is no time
Roof auto-estimate value. I had a number and wasn't sure. I asked a few people who came from the industry — former independent adjuster, former contractor, two former carrier leaders — not a wide net. They came back about double what we shipped. Valuable starting point. I still owned the call.

Loyalty stack
Family first. Then employees. Then everyone else. No blind loyalty except family. If a product is dying, try to resurrect it. If it can't be saved, move on and take as many people as you can. Some will not make the cut.

After we were acquired I was a project/product manager. Quality was bad. A close friend was QA manager. I fought for him about nine months and spent political capital. It was not going to turn around. If the new company fired him they might fire me for not doing my job. I let him go. I told him it wasn't working, that a fresh start would help him and the company, and that I would be a reference. He was a great contributor and not a great leader. I did not tell him I was under pressure. That burden was mine. I would do the conversation the same way today. I was blinded by friendship. I have kept more distance since. Don't be best friends with a subordinate. I can be loyal to my people and I cannot be loyal at the expense of my job or my family.

Honesty is not telling employees everything. I don't share every problem, emotion, or concern. There was no spin in that firing. The company needed a change and the decision was also best for me. Both can be true. I own the decision. Extreme Ownership named something I already did.

I will always choose what is best for me. The company will always choose what is best for it. "Work yourself out of a job" is lip service that helps the owner and misleads everyone else. I would not give that advice even if I owned the company. I would say work yourself into a promotion.

Political capital
It's ok to spend some if the longer-term win is worth it and you won't get fired for spending it. An underpaid good employee. Leaders above me didn't like him. Accepting their no would have looked better on me in the short term. I pushed. A few thousand dollars and some ruffled feathers. He got loyal and better. Short-term hit, long-term win. You don't win them all. When you miss, own it and make a repair plan with real interactions, not vibes.

2016–2020 international
We wanted a share of revenue from outside the US and Canada by 2025. I was VP on our second-biggest product and on the new product meant to get that revenue. I dropped the second-biggest — tens of millions a year — and concentrated on the new one, which had no revenue. That was the mistake. I should have stayed tied to the money and the future. Internal fights: I wanted a new estimating system; two execs wanted the new online product hooked to the old desktop system. I fought alone and lost. About two years of work got scrapped. Then COVID, a new CEO, strategy change, offices shut, product killed. I went from VP to product executive. Lost the team, the office, the parking spot. I told our president I would think about whether I still wanted to be there. Weekend of pride, identity, spite, anger, and money. I had fallen on the sword for decisions that were mine. What kept me was wanting to win and to show myself I was as good as I thought. I asked what I would want if I were in their shoes. The company owed me nothing. Every payday we were square. I faked a positive attitude until it was real. I hired someone from a competitor who wanted to be there. He taught me databases. We started an analytics team. I learned SQL. I pulled data out of Snowflake from my small office and it felt like I had conquered the world. Office and title stopped mattering. Past the pride, it was fun. That team grew into something another VP runs, much bigger. About a year later I was VP again on the biggest product. Since then new products that have beaten revenue projections, and the new online version. The big-office years, I was still leading, but I stopped learning. I don't want that again. I learn every week now. The motive is to get better at the job, not to signal.

Explaining tradeoffs (essay source)
Bloomington, late 90s or early 2000s. Claims customer. Spreadsheet and a date. I didn't know the iron triangle. I told them it can't all be done. Loud AC, fans, servers. We went through the list. I was blunt. They were a huge share of revenue and used to getting their way. They didn't like a new project manager telling them no. Further discussion, they appreciated the honesty. I stayed on that account from the product side for about ten years. What changed was not the spreadsheet. They were claims people. I walked them through QA, updating tens of thousands of laptops, how bad a revert is, how slow manual testing was. They became a partner. We moved them off "custom software shop" thinking and toward the generic product.

Utah: I said 3 months, a developer said 2. I told them my number included reverse-and-rebuild and late defects, and development was the raw number. After that, developers estimated, I padded, and only I gave the customer the date. Early on we dictated dates to developers. That was terrible. I stopped. Hitting the date mattered more than the length, because the people I worked with had bosses. Helping them look good for their bosses matters. If you are the single voice, you own the hit and you own the miss with humility.

Later we stopped custom features as the customer count grew. Agile, scheduled releases, educate the customer, ask for problems not features, solve for the industry. Better quality and predictability. We got vague on dates on purpose: a quarter, then a named monthly release as it got closer. Communicate constantly. Trust from hitting those. American Family era, vendor invoices, late defects in calculation code, postponed. They took it better because the date had not been carved in stone for 6–9 months. They cared that we cared about the integrity of the numbers.

Sometimes the game is gamesmanship, like allies who also spy, and both sides know. Farmers: a couple-week problem, then something smaller that mattered so one person could look good to her boss. I said we'd do the new thing and extend the bigger one. We actually finished both. Setting the longer expectation was the small risk. Promising both and missing the big one would have been worse.

Mitigation company, custom report, couldn't do it in their time because of how the code and the debt were. We were building other functionality we would charge for. I offered two free months of that and the report when we could. I told the analyst first. Then I told his boss he had pushed me hard. He knew it was theater. It made him look good. We're still friends. Everybody won, or everybody thought they won. If everyone thinks they won, then they did. New York Times test: I don't do deals I'd be afraid to have known. I could tell a customer we said a month because we are bad at forecasting and we would rather underpromise.

Tech debt: we try to spend about half our time on debt, tools, and code, half on customer-facing work. I keep that close to the vest. I've explained it and I usually avoid it. It gives me room to shift the percentage. I can't remember a time that secrecy saved or wrecked a relationship. The executive board is supposed to show that work. It's a work in progress. The board is automated from Jira. If it isn't healthy, it should show that. Dashboard theater is a net negative. Some cultures want theater. Ours, the president wants real data. A rare exception: a top-five customer in contract talks needed tracking on a new offering. Months of work. Other negotiations couldn't slip. We worked extra hours for about a month. I called the team in, we decided the details together, I put in the hours, we made it as fun as we could. We hadn't done that in a couple of years. Rare is the whole point. Keep it rare or you get the tyranny of the urgent and a jaded team. The execs had effectively already committed. The team owned the how, not the whether. Years of trust is why that didn't feel like manipulation.

Managing upward is harder than managing customers. CPO and president want to take care of customers. A chief claims officer or a CEO calls, they say we'll take care of it, and it becomes urgent. They sign the checks. The way through is a board everyone can see. If you can't show the work, the expectation is that you can do it all. Even then, a senior leader will get emotional about one idea, not know the commitments, and tell you to build it. Then they never mention it again. I ask: if I don't do this, does it reflect poorly on them because they already committed to a customer or their boss? If yes, I do it. If no, I don't.

Don't spend authority you don't have. As a PM I told a big insurer no on custom recoverable-depreciation calculations. Safety in doing it the way other insurers do it. Lawsuits happen when you are different. They complained. My boss said yes. He didn't have the history. He didn't mean to step on me. We built it. It is still a second path. Still tech debt. No lawsuit. He agreed afterward to talk to me before committing. Since then the culture is: nobody tells a customer we'll do it until it goes through product. A good failure.

QA used to be four weeks no matter what, and every fix restarted the clock. Excel test plans. Automation, and CI/CD on the newest online product, dissolved that. UX and discovery have not gotten shorter. Skipping UX is fine for a button color. When a PM or engineer invents the experience without research, we have had to redo it.

The art is knowing the customer and what drives the person. Most of them are mid-level. They care about doing a good job, money, and looking good for their boss. Win-wins are what I aspire to. You also have to know your own politics, your boss, your reports, and enough of the tech to speak intelligently. Mostly it is emotional intelligence. There aren't absolutes. Every situation is different. Same moral code, different relationships, like family versus friends.

I used to be rough. I can still be blunt, but I pick the time, and I tune it to the audience, their motivation, and their power structure. For years I heard the customer is always right, push people into overtime, make it work. That is lose-lose for them and for us. The change in me is that I got good at finding the win-win.

Football: I coached eight years. You can teach kids exact plays. You have more success teaching them the game. Why you block this guy. Why you leave that guy unblocked and read him. It's you versus the person lined up across from you. You win by learning what motivates them, their tendencies, their strengths and weaknesses. Scrum, Kanban, XP, SAFe, product operating model, waterfall — good things in all of them. The nuance, the tradeoffs, the politics, and reading people will outlast the model. When AI automates more of the work, those skills get more valuable, not less.

Changed my mind
- Customer is always right / make it work → find the win-win.
- Tie yourself to the future even if you drop current revenue → stay tied to both.
- Dictating dates to developers → they estimate, I carry a buffer I can explain, I own the date with the customer.
- Best friends with people who report to me → loyalty with distance.
- My product taste is enough → my taste is one data point.
- Work yourself out of a job → work yourself into a promotion. Still true if I owned the company.

Still unresolved
- Self-interest and "what would help if I were them" are both true. I don't have one sentence yet.
- Employees before customers, and also sometimes the customer has to win. I don't experience that as two moral codes.
- If everyone thinks they won, they won. I have not yet named a felt win that later turned out bad.
- "The company owes me nothing" may have stopped me from asking for something I should have negotiated. Not examined.
- How close is too close with direct reports, day to day.
- What I would drop first if the executive load and weekly learning actually collided.
