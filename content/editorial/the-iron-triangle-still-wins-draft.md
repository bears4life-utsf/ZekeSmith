# The Iron Triangle Still Wins

**Status:** Draft 1 · not published  
**Traces to:** Manifesto → Tradeoffs; Principle 2  
**Source:** Iron triangle interview, October 2026  
**Public rule:** scenes and costs are in. Names, accounts, dollar figures, headcount, and product names are out.  
**Left out on purpose:** the deprecated product; the automation beat; the late-1990s spreadsheet scene.

Why software leaders never escape the tradeoff between scope, speed, and quality.

---

I understood the constraint long before I had a name for it.

Twenty-five years ago I already knew that doing something too fast made it bad. Some work takes the time it takes. You can bake a cake for five minutes. It will not be a cake. You can try to build, in a day, a feature that needs a month. It will not be that feature. Those are extremes. They are still the picture.

Years later I learned the name. Scope, speed, and quality. You can decide two of them. You do not get the third for free. I had been living inside that fact for a long time. The name only made the arguments easier to recognize. Most of the arguments never used the words.

---

I once wrote a business case for a new product the company wanted. The case had the financials, the plan, and a request for a new team to build it. Leadership liked the idea. They also told me to build it with the people I already had. No new investment. No added staff.

I did not cut the scope of that product. Not then. I moved the date, and I reworked the financial story so the revenue started later and ran longer. I also told them what else would wait. The same people had been going to build an internal tool for our mobile product. That work would slip so this one could proceed.

They understood. With no new people, a longer road was the honest plan. I did not pretend the team we had could hold the original date, the full scope, and the quality bar at the same time. More people would have moved the date. They would not have deleted the choice. Something else would still have been waiting.

The pressure changed later. Competitors were close, and there was a real race to be first and to be right. The product had to move quickly, and it had to be good. So the first release got smaller. We shipped enough to prove the core workflow and learn. The sophisticated pieces, including the hard judgment customers said they actually wanted, waited for a later phase.

I was afraid those customers, or a competitor, would make a point of what we had left out. In practice we were further ahead than I thought. Nobody else had that judgment either. It was a genuinely hard problem. The fear was useful. It kept us from getting casual. It was not, in the end, a description of the market.

The cost was still real. Some early customers chose to wait for the second phase. They wanted the judgment we had postponed. We did not have it yet, so they did not buy. We added that scope behind the first release, and the product worked. Releasing a smaller version faster was the right call. It was not a free one. A few buyers spent their patience instead of their money, and we had to earn them later.

There was a second cost I did not see on the day we cut scope. We had a price in mind for the full product. I no longer believed the smaller release could hold it. I went to customers, sat with them, showed them what we actually had, and asked what they would pay. I also asked marketing to look again.

We lowered the price. Then one of those customers named a number above the price we were about to charge. I told them we could come in under that. Other conversations landed in the same range. The smaller product had value. My fear that it did not was wrong. The price still moved, because the thing we were selling was no longer the thing we had originally described.

That is the triangle without the vocabulary. Speed and quality held. Scope gave. Price gave with it. The people who wanted the postponed judgment paid in waiting. We paid in a cheaper first sale. Nobody in those meetings said scope, speed, or quality. We talked about a race, a phase, and a number.

---

A different release taught me the opposite corner.

We were close to shipping a feature, and we discovered we had made a mistake in the idea itself. Using it could put our customers, large insurance carriers, in front of lawyers. Carriers are sued often. Difference is one of the things they get sued over. Shipping the feature as it stood was not a small defect. It was a risk we would be handing to the people who paid us.

Sales wanted to release it and fix it quietly later. The dates were already in the market. Holding the release would frustrate customers, threaten deals, and make the people who had promised those dates look unreliable.

I held it. We announced the postponement, took the criticism, and changed the feature before it shipped. It was my call.

Sales was partly right. Customers were angry. Smaller ones held off on buying. Some of the embarrassment they had predicted showed up, because they had already told the market when the thing was coming.

The larger carriers heard the delay differently. We were postponing the feature to protect them. I went out with the sales team, owned the decision, and explained why. Most customers understood. A lot of them were glad someone had said it plainly.

Quality held. The date gave. Sales paid in awkward conversations, and some smaller buyers walked. I would make the same call again. The part I would repeat on purpose is the part after the decision: do not leave sales to explain a delay they argued against. Stand there with them and say why.

---

The triangle does not retire because the people in the room are more senior, or because the work is more urgent.

Not long ago an executive sent a demand. This outcome, by this date. No trade. The team that had to do it was already on other high-priority work. They were specialists. We could not hand the new work to someone else and keep moving. I said so. I said that if we forced both, quality on one of them would give.

The answer was to get it done.

We hit the dates. The quality on one feature was bad, and we had to hotfix it. The repair cost a weekend, extra hours, frustration, and context switching. Other work moved because those people were no longer on it. I had named the tradeoff before the date. It happened anyway. Refusing to choose did not protect scope and speed and quality. It chose quality as the thing that would break, and it sent the bill to the team.

---

I still get asked, in one form or another, to keep all three. A new team will hold the date. A smaller first release will not really cost us. We can ship now and fix it quietly. We can just get it done. Sometimes the fear behind the request is wrong. Competitors were not as far ahead as I thought. The smaller product was worth buying. Sometimes the fear is right. A feature can put a customer in court. A date can be hit by people who then spend the weekend repairing what they shipped.

What has never happened, in my experience, is the third miracle: scope, speed, and quality, all held, because we wanted them badly enough. More people move the date. A smaller release moves the price and asks some customers to wait. Protecting a customer moves the date and costs you deals. A direct order to ignore the choice moves the pain onto the people who have to hotfix the result.

You can decide two. The third one is decided for you, whether or not anyone in the room is willing to say so.
