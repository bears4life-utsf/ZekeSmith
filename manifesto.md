## Core Beliefs

Fundamental beliefs about software leadership. These are the assumptions everything else rests on.

Format each as a single declarative sentence. Add the reasoning underneath only if the sentence needs defending.

1. Frameworks are plays; reading motivation, power, and cost is the game.
2. The company and the person each choose what is best for themselves, and pretending otherwise is lip service.
3. Honesty, integrity, and humility are required before any political skill is safe.
4. Every operating model eventually optimizes for something, and a leader’s job is to notice when that something is no longer the customer’s problem.
5. Process exists to solve customer problems. When the process becomes the thing being served, the model has inverted.
6. Ownership without the authority to affect the outcome creates learned helplessness.
7. A leader’s taste is one data point, and it is often a weak one, because familiarity hides what a new user feels.
8. Customers do not care about the operating model. They care whether the problem gets solved, and whether the person they deal with can look competent to their own boss.
9. Some costs are temporary. A four-week QA clock that restarted on every fix was once physics, and automation dissolved it. Discovery and real UX have not.
10. Learning is a personal operating requirement. Status can leave someone still leading and no longer growing.
11. Family comes first, then employees, then everyone else. Employee loyalty is real and not blind.

> Which of your beliefs would a smart, experienced peer disagree with? Those are the ones worth stating.
> Which beliefs have you changed your mind about? Record the old version too.
> Which of these could you defend with evidence, and which are currently convictions?

**Beliefs changed, old version kept:**

- Old: the customer is always right; make it work, including by pushing people into overtime. New: that pattern is often lose-lose. Look for a win both sides actually experience as a win.
- Old: tie yourself to the future even if it means dropping current revenue. New: stay tied to money that exists and to the bet that will become money. Dropping a product that made tens of millions for a product with no revenue was the expensive lesson.
- Old: work yourself out of a job. New: work yourself into a promotion. This stays rejected even from the owner’s side of the table.

**Still in tension, not quietly overwritten:**

- Operating models should optimize for the customer’s problem. Personal loyalty still runs family, then employees, then everyone else. Sometimes the customer must win because that is in the employees’ interest. These are not two moral codes in practice, and they will read as a conflict until they are one sentence.

## Principles

A growing list of leadership principles. Beliefs describe how the world is; principles describe how you act.

Suggested format:

**Principle name** — What to do. When it applies. What it costs.

1. **Teach the game** — Show why this player gets blocked and why that one gets read. Use a framework as a play, not as the answer. Costs time, and it refuses the comfort of a single method.
2. **Say the cost out loud** — Any recommendation names what it gives up. When a date, a scope cut, or a quality slip is the cost, say which one. Costs the feeling of having a clean answer.
3. **Challenge your own taste first** — Treat your preference as one data point. When there is time, run the disagreement as an experiment. When there is not, ask a few people who actually represent the segments, then own the call. Costs speed and the pleasure of being right.
4. **Promise only what you can explain** — Developers estimate. Carry a buffer for forecasting error, rollback, and late defects. Tell the customer a date you can defend out loud, then narrow it as uncertainty drops. Costs optimism, and sometimes it leaves capacity unused.
5. **Do the urgent thing only when someone’s reputation is already attached** — If an executive has committed to a customer or to their own boss, do the work. If they are only hot about an idea, let it die. Costs the appearance of being instantly responsive.
6. **Make the real work visible** — Keep committed work, including foundation work, on an automated board leaders actually use. A flattering dashboard is worse than none. Costs the politics of a pretty slide, and it removes the excuse of infinite capacity.
7. **Spend authority you actually have** — Do not tell a customer no, or yes, if someone above you will reverse it. Clear customer commitments through the people who must live with them. Costs speed, and it can look like hesitation.
8. **Own the decision in the room** — Praise the team. Take the failure. Do not hand the affected person your political pressure as the reason. When executives decide with you, write it down so they remain teammates if it fails. Costs pride, and it forbids hiding behind “the company.”
9. **Keep crunch rare, and be in it** — Sometimes the work all has to happen. Do it with the team, on the how, and do not let it become the operating system. Costs heroics as an identity.
10. **Stay tied to money and to the future** — Do not trade a product that pays the bills for a bet with no revenue, and do not cling to a dying product out of ego. If the baby is ugly, say so. Costs concentration on a single exciting story.
11. **Protect craft, including the IC path** — Pay it, title it, and say it out loud. Do not treat management as the only promotion. Costs headcount plans that assume every strong person should become a manager.
12. **Work yourself into a promotion** — Get more valuable at a bigger problem. Do not try to become unnecessary. Costs the owner-flavored story that efficiency means erasing your own job.
13. **Keep learning after the title arrives** — Build something every week. Status, travel, and a big office can leave the leadership intact and the growth dead. Costs time that executive work will always try to take.
14. **Keep a fence around friendship** — Be loyal to people who report to you. Do not become best friends with someone you may have to remove. Family still outranks the job. Costs warmth, and it is the price of being able to decide.

When these conflict: family outranks employees, and employees outrank everyone else, unless doing the customer’s thing is genuinely in the employees’ interest. A shared, written executive decision outranks a solo act of ownership. A date you can hit outranks a date that sounds brave. An experiment outranks taste when there is time to run one.

> Does each principle tell someone what to *do* differently on Monday?
> What does each one cost when followed? A principle with no cost is a slogan.
> Which principles conflict with each other? Say which wins and when.

## Tradeoffs

Why tradeoffs are central to this philosophy.

I have used Scrum, Kanban, Extreme Programming, SAFe, a product operating model, and waterfall. There is something worth keeping in each of them. None of them is the game. You can teach people to run an exact play. You have more success when you teach them why they block one player and read another, and that the person across from them has motives, tendencies, and a boss. A leader who can see the cost — of a date, a custom feature, a ceremony, a yes said above the team — can negotiate a win both sides recognize. A leader who only has a framework gets surprised every time the framework starts optimizing for itself. Some costs dissolve. The month-long manual QA clock did. The time it takes to understand a human problem has not, and reading power and motivation will matter more as more of the building is automated. Say the tradeoff in prose when the lesson is a story, in a diagram when the inversion should be obvious at a glance, and in an interactive model when the reader needs to feel one choice move the others. The model shows tension. It does not score an organization or pick the answer.

> Why tradeoffs rather than best practices, frameworks, or maturity models?
> What does a leader gain by learning to see tradeoffs they cannot otherwise get?
> What are the tradeoffs you consider permanent, and which ones does technology actually dissolve?
> How do you want tradeoffs represented — prose, diagrams, interactive models? What does each form do well?

**Standing rule for all work in this repository:** if a piece of writing recommends a course of action, it must also name what that action costs.

## Writing Standards

How this work should feel to read.

- Plain English over consultant jargon
- Quiet confidence
- Timeless over trendy
- Evidence before opinion
- Stories that teach rather than impress
- Distinguish evidence from interpretation
- Present counterarguments honestly
- Admit uncertainty when appropriate

**Additional standards:**

- The gray-area moves may be written in plain language: a story the other person already agreed to, a date you expect to beat, an executive idea you let die because no reputation is attached to it. The line that makes the move defensible is part of the writing, not a footnote.
- Names, account names, and live deals stay off the public page. The pattern is the lesson. The cast is not.

## Confidentiality Standards

How internal company experience becomes a public lesson.

**Never published:** names of people in a deal, customer or account names, current negotiations, revenue figures used as proof, and anything a current colleague or customer could recognize as their own meeting.

**Transformation rules:** keep the move and the reason it was legitimate. Drop the cast. A recoverable lesson is one you could explain in a room without pointing at the people who were in the original room.

**Review step:** the New York Times test. If you would be afraid for the pattern to be known, it does not ship. If you would be comfortable with the pattern and uncomfortable with the names, publish the pattern.

> What is the test that separates "a lesson I learned" from "a story that is not mine to tell"?
> Would you be comfortable if a former colleague read this and recognized the situation?
> When is a detail load-bearing for the lesson, and when is it just color that should be cut?
> Who reads the draft when you are unsure?
